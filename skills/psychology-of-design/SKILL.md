# Psychology of Design

A working reference of 50 principles from behavioral science and cognitive psychology, framed for product and UX design (sourced from the Growth.Design "Psychology of Design" catalog). Each detailed principle includes what it is, how it shows up in real products, and a short checklist for applying it.

Use this to ground design decisions and critiques in *how people actually perceive, decide, and act* — instead of generic "make it cleaner" advice.

## When to use this skill

Pull these principles in whenever the work touches human behavior in an interface, including when the user:

- Designs or reviews a screen, landing page, signup/onboarding flow, pricing page, checkout, email, or notification.
- Asks why users drop off, hesitate, churn, ignore something, or fail to convert.
- Wants something more persuasive, intuitive, motivating, trustworthy, or easier to use.
- Asks for a design critique or UX feedback, or for the *rationale* behind a product decision.

You don't need the user to name a principle. "Why is no one clicking my CTA?" is a Fitts's Law / contrast / visual-hierarchy question. "Too many people abandon signup" is a Hick's Law / cognitive-load / progressive-disclosure question.

## How to apply it

1. **Diagnose the behavior first.** Name the human behavior that's the problem or goal — *decision paralysis, low motivation, lack of trust, missed attention, high effort.* Don't start from the principle; start from what the user is doing or failing to do.
2. **Pick the matching category.** Use the index below. *Information* principles govern attention, comprehension, and decision effort. *Meaning* principles govern motivation, trust, value, and behavior change. Many problems span both.
3. **Read the detail before prescribing.** Open the relevant reference file (`references/information-principles.md` or `references/meaning-principles.md`) and read the full entry — definition, real-product examples, and the application checklist — rather than relying on the one-line summary.
4. **Recommend concretely.** Tie each suggestion to the principle and to a specific change in *their* design ("Group these 15 dropdown options into 3 categories — Hick's Law: decision time grows with the number of choices"). Cite 2–4 principles at most; don't dump the whole catalog.
5. **Pick the strongest lever, not every lever.** A good critique applies a few well-matched principles deeply, not twenty shallowly.

## Using it ethically

These are genuine influence mechanisms, which means they can be used to help users or to manipulate them. Apply them to reduce friction and help people reach outcomes *they* want — not to trick, pressure, or trap. Be especially careful with scarcity, social proof, anchoring, variable reward, and decoy effects: fabricated scarcity ("only 2 left!" when untrue), fake social proof, and manipulative urgency are dark patterns. If a request amounts to deceiving or coercing users, say so and offer an honest alternative that achieves the legitimate goal.

## The catalog

Two categories. **Bold** entries have full write-ups (definition, examples, checklist) in the reference files. Entries marked *(name only)* appear in the source without detail — treat them as concepts to recall or research, not to invent specifics about.

Read `references/information-principles.md` or `references/meaning-principles.md` for the full detail on any bold entry.

### Information — attention, perception, comprehension, decision effort

- **👀 Hick's Law** — More options leads to harder decisions
- **💼 Confirmation Bias** — People look for evidence that confirms what they think
- **👁 Priming** — Previous stimuli influence users' decisions
- **🚛 Cognitive Load** — Total mental effort required to complete a task
- **⚓️ Anchoring Bias** — Users rely heavily on the first piece of information they see
- **👉 Nudge** — Subtle hints can affect users' decisions
- **🍰 Progressive Disclosure** — Users are less overwhelmed if complex features come later
- **🎯 Fitts's Law** — Large and close elements are easier to interact with
- **🕶 Banner Blindness** — Users tune out things they're repeatedly exposed to
- **🚨 External Trigger** — When the prompt itself tells users what to do next
- **🕺 Decoy Effect** — A new option that's easy to discard makes another look better
- **🖼 Framing** — How information is presented affects decisions
- 🐠 Attentional Bias — Thoughts filter what people pay attention to *(name only)*
- 💔 Empathy Gap — People underestimate how much emotion drives behavior *(name only)*
- ⛵️ Visual Anchors — Elements used to guide users' eyes *(name only)*
- 🌶 Von Restorff Effect — People notice items that stand out *(name only)*
- 🎖 Visual Hierarchy — The order in which people perceive what they see *(name only)*
- 🔭 Selective Attention — People filter out things when focused *(name only)*
- ✈️ Survivorship Bias — People neglect what didn't pass a selection process *(name only)*
- 🍒 Juxtaposition — Close, similar elements read as one unit *(name only)*
- 🚦 Signifiers — Elements that communicate what they'll do *(name only)*
- 🎭 Contrast — Attention is drawn to higher visual weight *(name only)*
- 🎪 Centre-Stage Effect — People tend to pick the middle option *(name only)*
- 🍣 Law of Proximity — Elements close together are seen as related *(name only)*
- 🍬 Tesler's Law — Over-simplifying transfers complexity to users *(name only)*
- 🧨 Spark Effect — Users act more when the effort is small *(name only)*
- 🥏 Feedback Loop — Feedback communicates what an action did *(name only)*
- 😻 Expectations Bias — People are influenced by their own expectations *(name only)*
- 🚆 Aesthetic-Usability Effect — Beautiful designs feel easier to use *(name only)*

### Meaning — motivation, trust, value, behavior change

- **👥 Social Proof** — Users adapt behavior based on what others do
- **🦄 Scarcity** — People value things more when supply is limited
- **💭 Curiosity Gap** — Users want to seek out missing information
- **🖲 Mental Model** — Users have preconceived ideas of how things work
- **👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Familiarity Bias** — People prefer familiar experiences
- **🕹 Skeuomorphism** — Real-world-looking things are easier to adopt
- **🎁 Reciprocity** — People feel the need to give back when they receive
- **🤝 Singularity Effect** — One individual moves people more than a group
- **🎰 Variable Reward** — People especially enjoy unexpected rewards
- **🎉 Aha! moment** — When new users first realize your product's value
- **🥅 Goal Gradient Effect** — Motivation rises as users near a goal
- **💈 Occam's Razor** — Simple solutions often beat complex ones
- **🎗 Noble Edge Effect** — Users prefer socially responsible companies
- **🧿 Hawthorne Effect** — People change behavior when they know they're observed
- 👼 Halo Effect — People judge the whole from one trait *(name only)*
- ☎️ Miller's Law — Working memory holds about 7±2 items *(name only)*
- 🍱 Unit Bias — One unit feels like the right amount *(name only)*
- 🌊 Flow State — Being fully immersed in a task *(name only)*
- 👑 Authority Bias — Authority figures' opinions carry extra weight *(name only)*
- 🏺 Pseudo-Set Framing — Tasks framed as a set are more tempting to finish *(name only)*
- 🎊 Group Attractiveness Effect — Items look better shown in a group *(name only)*
